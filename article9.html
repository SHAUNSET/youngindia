FROM CYBERWARFARE TO AI-ENABLED CYBER OPERATIONS: PREPARING INDIA'S CYBERSECURITY ARCHITECTURE FOR AN AGENTIC AGE

BY SHAUNAK NAIK – YOUNG INDIA
AUGUST 8 , 2026

AI, Cybersecurity & India's Strategic Resilience

INTRODUCTION

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as one of the fastest-growing technologies of the twenty-first century, driving competition among global powers over critical mineral supply chains, semiconductor manufacturing, and the development of increasingly capable Large Language Models (LLMs). According to the KPMG Global Tech Report, a significant share of organisations have begun integrating AI agents into core business processes, signalling a shift from experimental deployment to operational reliance. AI is therefore no longer confined to scientific laboratories or technology firms; it is steadily shaping government services, large-scale businesses, defence supply chains, and everyday personal life. This transition suggests that AI is no longer simply a technological innovation but is gradually becoming a strategic layer across national economies and governance systems.

Recent advancements in Anthropic's Claude model family highlight the growing capability of LLMs to perform complex, multi-step tasks in domains such as cybersecurity, software engineering, and research workflows. The company has noted that the model's strong performance in cybersecurity-related tasks emerged as an unintended by-product of improvements in coding and reasoning abilities. Such developments demonstrate how rapid advances in AI capabilities can generate outcomes that were not explicitly anticipated during development. As AI systems become more capable, distinguishing between beneficial and potentially risky applications is becoming harder.

The growing use of AI in cybersecurity presents a classic dual-use dilemma. On one hand, AI can help identify software vulnerabilities, strengthen authentication and encryption systems, and detect suspicious network activity before significant damage occurs. On the other, the same capabilities can be exploited to automate phishing campaigns, accelerate malware development, and identify weaknesses across digital systems at a scale and speed that was previously difficult to achieve. The technology itself is therefore not inherently offensive or defensive; its impact depends largely on who controls it and the safeguards governing its deployment.

For a rapidly digitising country like India, where governance, financial systems, and public service delivery are becoming increasingly dependent on digital infrastructure, these developments extend beyond a technical challenge. They raise broader questions about cybersecurity, data protection, and national resilience. As AI capabilities continue to evolve, the policy challenge is no longer limited to adopting AI responsibly, but also to ensuring that existing cybersecurity frameworks evolve at the same pace as the technologies they are designed to secure.

To understand why this challenge is becoming more urgent, it is necessary to examine how cyber operations themselves have evolved over the past decade.

FROM CYBERWARFARE TO AI-ENABLED CYBER OPERATIONS

According to data compiled by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), state-sponsored cyber operations have become an increasingly prominent instrument of geopolitical competition. Chinese cyber groups reportedly conduct millions of cyber intrusion attempts against Taiwanese government and telecommunications networks each day, while Russian cyber operations have accompanied conventional military actions in Ukraine by targeting critical infrastructure, including energy, communications, and defence systems. These developments demonstrate that cyberspace is no longer merely a technical domain but an important arena of strategic competition, where states pursue political, military, and economic objectives below the threshold of conventional warfare. As geopolitical competition extends further into the digital domain, cyber capabilities are becoming as strategically relevant as conventional military assets.

As cyber operations assume greater strategic importance, governments and organisations are seeking technologies that can improve the speed, scale, and effectiveness of cyber capabilities. Frontier AI systems have emerged within this broader shift. Recent advances in large language models have expanded their ability to analyse software, identify vulnerabilities, generate code, and assist in complex technical workflows. While these capabilities can significantly strengthen cyber defence, they also carry an inherent dual-use character. The same models that help security teams detect vulnerabilities can also lower the technical barriers for identifying and exploiting them if deployed with malicious intent.

Anthropic's Mythos-class models illustrate this evolving reality. According to the company's system documentation, the models demonstrate advanced capabilities in vulnerability discovery, software analysis, and multi-step cyber-related workflows. Recognising both their utility and potential risks, Anthropic has restricted access through Project Glasswing, making the models available only to selected government cyber-defenders, critical infrastructure operators, and trusted partners. This cautious deployment reflects a broader recognition within the frontier AI community that model capabilities are advancing faster than the governance frameworks required to manage them safely.

Among the most consequential developments is the emergence of agentic autonomy : the ability of AI systems to plan, execute, evaluate, and adapt multi-step tasks with limited human intervention. In practical terms, such systems can pursue a specified objective, assess intermediate outcomes, learn from failures, and modify their approach in pursuit of the desired result.

Traditionally, conducting a sophisticated cyber intrusion required significant technical expertise, coordination among multiple actors, and considerable time. Reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, credential acquisition, lateral movement, and data exfiltration were labour-intensive activities carried out by skilled human operators. Advanced AI systems have the potential to reduce some of these barriers by automating portions of the cyber kill chain and accelerating tasks that previously demanded substantial human effort. Although current frontier models remain far from fully autonomous cyber operators, they represent a clear shift from AI as a passive assistant towards AI as an active participant in complex cyber workflows. This shift matters because cyber operations have traditionally relied not only on technical skill but also on continuous human decision-making throughout an attack.

In a world where data has become a strategic asset and cyber operations increasingly complement conventional military and economic competition, securing digital infrastructure is no longer solely a technical challenge but a matter of national security. For a demographically rich and rapidly digitising country like India, where governance, financial systems, and critical public infrastructure are becoming deeply dependent on digital networks, protecting national data assets assumes strategic importance. As frontier AI capabilities continue to evolve, the central policy question is no longer whether cyber threats will become more sophisticated, but whether existing cybersecurity frameworks are evolving quickly enough to keep pace with them.

INDIA'S EXISTING CYBERSECURITY ARCHITECTURE: LAYERED DEFENCES AND COMPLIANCE FRAMEWORKS

India's cybersecurity architecture for digital public infrastructure and e-governance follows a defence-in-depth approach, combining regulatory standards, technical safeguards, and incident-response mechanisms. Instead of relying on a single security barrier, the framework establishes multiple layers of protection across the lifecycle of a digital system, from software development and deployment to user authentication, data protection, and post-incident recovery. This layered architecture is primarily anchored in the Guidelines for Indian Government Websites (GIGW) 3.0, CERT-In advisories, Safe-to-Host certification requirements, and periodic security audits conducted by empanelled agencies. Collectively, these frameworks aim to reduce the attack surface available to malicious actors while ensuring that government digital services remain secure, reliable, and resilient.

At the perimeter level, government websites and digital services are required to implement secure hosting environments and robust network security controls before they are exposed to the public internet. GIGW 3.0 and Safe-to-Host requirements mandate periodic security assessments to identify vulnerabilities and verify compliance with established cybersecurity standards. These measures are designed to minimise exposure to commonly exploited attack vectors and reduce the likelihood of unauthorised access at the earliest stage of a cyber intrusion.

Security is further reinforced at the application layer. Government portals and digital applications are expected to undergo vulnerability assessments and security audits before deployment, with particular emphasis on addressing widely exploited weaknesses identified under frameworks such as the OWASP Top 10. Through mandatory code reviews, penetration testing, and compliance audits, the framework seeks to eliminate vulnerabilities such as SQL injection, cross-site scripting, insecure APIs, and other common software flaws before they can be exploited in operational environments.

Beyond application security, India's cybersecurity posture relies heavily on identity and access management controls. CERT-In advisories recommend multi-factor authentication, role-based access controls, and the principle of least privilege to reduce the risk of unauthorised access and limit lateral movement within compromised systems. Sensitive information is further protected through encryption protocols for data both in transit and at rest, while Data Loss Prevention (DLP) mechanisms help prevent the unauthorised extraction of sensitive government and citizen data. Together, these controls are intended not only to keep attackers out but also to restrict the damage they can cause if an intrusion occurs.

The framework also incorporates dedicated incident detection and response mechanisms. CERT-In guidelines emphasise continuous monitoring, real-time alert generation, forensic preservation, and coordinated incident-response procedures. Organisations are advised to maintain dedicated response teams capable of containing breaches while preserving digital evidence for subsequent forensic investigation. Recognising that no cybersecurity system is entirely breach-proof, these measures focus on limiting operational disruption, enabling rapid recovery, and strengthening institutional resilience following an attack.

Taken together, these measures show that India's cybersecurity architecture has evolved into a layered, standards-based defence model against conventional cyber threats. By combining preventive, detective, and responsive security controls, the framework seeks to counter credential theft, software vulnerabilities, phishing campaigns, and other forms of human-operated cyber intrusions. However, this architecture has largely evolved around threat models in which human actors remain at the centre of cyber operations. Whether these assumptions continue to hold in an era of increasingly autonomous, AI-enabled cyber capabilities is the question that now demands closer examination.

WHEN AI MEETS INDIA'S EXISTING CYBERSECURITY ARCHITECTURE

India's existing cybersecurity architecture provides multiple layers of protection against conventional cyber threats. However, most of these controls are designed around a relatively familiar threat model: an attacker identifies a vulnerability, attempts to exploit it, gains access, and then moves through the system. The growing capabilities of frontier AI systems could alter this model, not necessarily by making existing security controls obsolete, but by making it possible to discover and exploit weaknesses faster and across multiple stages of an attack.

The first major change is the speed and scale of vulnerability discovery. Anthropic's Project Glasswing offers an early indication of what this could mean. Anthropic reported that Claude Mythos Preview identified thousands of high-severity vulnerabilities across critical software, and that its approximately fifty initial partners had identified more than ten thousand high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities within the first weeks of deployment. The significance of this development is not simply that AI can find vulnerabilities; security researchers have used automated tools for this purpose for years. The difference lies in the growing ability of frontier models to understand complex software, reason through vulnerabilities, generate code, and work through technical problems with limited human guidance. If defenders can use these capabilities to identify weaknesses before deployment, attackers could potentially use the same capabilities to search for weaknesses at a much larger scale.

This creates a problem for a framework such as GIGW 3.0 and Safe-to-Host. A security audit can establish that a system meets a defined set of security requirements at a particular point in time, but the threat environment does not remain static. An AI-enabled attacker does not necessarily need to find an entirely new way into a system; it may simply identify a small weakness that was missed during testing, analyse the surrounding software, and repeatedly search for another path when the first attempt fails. In other words, the challenge may shift from whether a system contains vulnerabilities to whether defenders can identify and patch them faster than increasingly capable AI systems can discover them.

A second challenge arises from agentic autonomy. Traditional cybersecurity controls are often designed to stop individual actions: unauthorised access, malicious code, suspicious network traffic, or attempts to exploit a known vulnerability. An AI agent, however, can potentially combine several individually permissible actions into a larger sequence directed towards a single objective. OWASP's Top 10 for Agentic Applications identifies risks including agent goal hijacking, tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, and agentic supply-chain vulnerabilities. These risks matter because an agent does not necessarily have to defeat every security layer directly; it may instead exploit the connections between them.

For example, a system may correctly enforce authentication, restrict user privileges, and monitor network activity as separate security measures. Yet if an AI system is given legitimate access to several tools or services, manipulating the agent into using those tools in an unintended sequence could create a new attack path. This is fundamentally different from the traditional model of a hacker simply “breaking through” a firewall. The concern is instead about how legitimate permissions, tools, and information can be combined by an autonomous system to produce an illegitimate outcome. OWASP's identification of tool misuse and identity and privilege abuse as distinct agentic risks reflects this shift in the threat model.

The third concern is that AI can make human-facing security controls themselves part of the attack surface. Conventional cybersecurity focuses heavily on securing software, networks, credentials, and servers. AI-enabled systems introduce another layer: the instructions and information on which an AI agent bases its decisions. Prompt injection, for instance, can manipulate an AI system through malicious instructions embedded in user input or external content. OWASP identifies prompt injection, excessive agency, and sensitive information disclosure among the major risks associated with LLM-based systems.

This becomes particularly relevant when AI agents are connected to government databases, internal applications, or other tools. A vulnerability does not necessarily have to exist in the underlying database itself; the agent connecting to that database can become the point through which the system is manipulated. This represents an important shift from securing only the infrastructure to also securing the decision-making layer that sits on top of it.

The concern, therefore, is not that AI will simply “break” India's existing cybersecurity architecture. Rather, AI could expose a gap between what existing security frameworks are designed to defend against and how future attacks may actually be conducted. India's current measures provide important protection against known software vulnerabilities, unauthorised access, and conventional intrusion techniques. However, they were largely developed around systems where human operators make the decisions at each stage of an attack. Frontier AI systems are beginning to challenge that assumption by combining vulnerability discovery, reasoning, tool use, and multi-step execution within a single workflow. Anthropic's decision to restrict Mythos 5 to a small group of trusted partners, while separately maintaining stronger cyber safeguards on its generally available model, itself reflects the seriousness with which the industry now views these capabilities.

For India, therefore, the emerging challenge is not to replace its existing cybersecurity architecture but to extend it to account for AI-enabled threats. The next question is how existing frameworks such as GIGW 3.0, CERT-In advisories, and Safe-to-Host requirements can be supplemented with controls designed specifically for autonomous AI systems, particularly around agent permissions, tool access, AI-generated actions, continuous testing, and the integrity of the data on which these systems operate.

BRIDGING THE GAP: SECURING INDIA AGAINST AI-ENABLED CYBER THREATS

The answer is not to replace India's existing cybersecurity architecture but to build an additional layer around it. GIGW 3.0, CERT-In advisories, and existing security audits remain important because AI does not make conventional vulnerabilities disappear. However, increasingly autonomous systems introduce new risks that traditional security controls were not designed to address. An AI agent may not need to exploit a conventional vulnerability directly; it could manipulate the information it receives, misuse an authorised tool, or combine several legitimate actions in a way that creates an unintended security breach. OWASP's emerging guidance on agentic AI identifies risks across areas such as agent autonomy, tool use, memory, identity, and human oversight, highlighting how the attack surface changes once AI systems are given the ability to act rather than simply respond.

India could therefore begin by introducing AI-specific security assessments for government systems that deploy autonomous or semi-autonomous agents. Existing audits largely ask whether a system is secure against known vulnerabilities; future assessments would also need to ask what an AI system can access, what actions it is authorised to take, and what happens if its instructions or decision-making process are manipulated. This could include testing for prompt injection, unauthorised tool use, manipulation of an agent's memory or knowledge base, and excessive autonomy before such systems are deployed in sensitive government environments.

The principle of least privilege should also be extended from human users to AI agents. An agent handling a citizen-service portal, for instance, should not automatically have access to databases, administrative controls, or external APIs simply because those tools are technically available to it. Access should instead be limited to the minimum permissions required for a particular task, with sensitive actions requiring additional verification. Such controls would ensure that even if an agent is manipulated, the damage remains contained rather than allowing one compromised system to move freely across an entire digital network.

India should also develop stronger human-in-the-loop controls for high-impact actions. An AI system may be allowed to detect a vulnerability, investigate an unusual login, or recommend that a compromised account be isolated. Actions such as deleting databases, changing access privileges, transferring sensitive information, or modifying critical infrastructure, however, should require human approval. This is particularly important because the main advantage of agentic AI, its ability to continue acting without waiting for instructions, can also become its biggest security weakness if the system makes an incorrect decision at scale.

Finally, cybersecurity testing will need to move beyond conventional penetration testing towards continuous testing of AI-enabled systems. OWASP's recent guidance on securing agentic applications recommends treating an agent's reasoning, memory, tools, and interactions as part of the security surface rather than focusing only on the underlying software. For India, this could mean creating dedicated red-team exercises for government AI systems, where security teams deliberately attempt to manipulate agents and test whether they can be made to exceed their authorised objectives. The goal should not be to build a completely separate cybersecurity architecture for AI, but to make the existing one capable of defending against systems that can increasingly think, adapt, and act on their own.

The broader challenge for India, therefore, is one of keeping pace. The country has spent years building standards to secure websites, applications, networks, and data against human-driven cyber threats. The next stage will require securing the layer that increasingly sits between these systems and the people operating them: the AI agent itself.

CONCLUSION

The challenge before India is not simply to defend its digital infrastructure against more sophisticated cyberattacks, but to prepare for a shift in who, or what, can conduct them. As AI systems move from assisting human operators to independently carrying out more complex tasks, the boundary between a cyber tool and a cyber actor is becoming less clear.

India's existing cybersecurity architecture provides a strong foundation, but its effectiveness will increasingly depend on how quickly it adapts to this changing threat environment. The objective should be to ensure that the country's growing adoption of AI does not create a new vulnerability within the very digital infrastructure that AI is expected to strengthen.

The next phase of India's cybersecurity preparedness, therefore, will be determined not only by how well it protects its systems from today's attackers, but by whether it is prepared for the possibility that tomorrow's attackers may not need to be human.

"The future of cybersecurity will not only be about securing systems from people, but about securing systems that can increasingly act on their own."
